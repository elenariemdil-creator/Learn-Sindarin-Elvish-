01

Vowels

Before you begin learning vocabulary or grammar, you need to be able to pronounce the words you encounter. Start with these six vowel sounds.

A / Á / Â

Use the open sound in father, never the sound in hat. Accents indicate how long the vowel is held.

E / É / Ê

Always like the e in fed or head, never the sound in feet.

I / Í / Î

An unaccented i is short, as in sick or hit. An accented i is a long “ee,” as in machine. At the beginning of a word before another vowel, it becomes a “y” sound: iaur (ancient), iant (bridge), iuitha- (to use).

O / Ó / Ô

A short o is like the vowel in dog or lot. The accented forms are described in this guide as the sound in snow or oh.

U / Ú / Û

Like the “oo” in tool. See the note on short vowels below for Tolkien’s more specific guidance.

Y / Ý / Ŷ

A rounded vowel, like the French u in lune. Try saying “ee” while rounding your lips.

02

Consonants

Most consonants are familiar. These are the sounds that need special attention.

C

Always hard, as in kite, never soft as in cent or piece.

CH

As in Scots loch or German Bach, never English church.

DH

The voiced “th” in these or those, not thing or thief.

F

At the beginning of a word, “f” as in father. Elsewhere, a “v” sound as in verge.

G

Always hard as in get, never soft as in edge.

HW

A breathy “w,” like the older British pronunciation of white. Imagine reversing the letters WH.

I

A “y” sound as in yacht when it begins a word before a vowel.

L

Clear as in let or leaf, not the “dark L” heard in British English bottle.

LH

The Welsh LL sound, as in llan. For an approximation, think of the letters as HL.

NG

At the beginning or end of a word, as in ring or sing. Within a word, as in finger.

PH

An “f” sound, as in phonic or phone.

R

Trilled, as in Spanish.

RH

A voiceless trilled R at the beginning of words. Imagine a breathy H followed by a lightly trilled R.

TH

The unvoiced “th” in thing or thick, never the voiced sound in the or that.

W

Normally as in wet or woe. At the end of a word, Elves retained a W without adding a vowel after it; Gondorians pronounced it like Sindarin U.

Keep the ending clean. The schwa—the unstressed vowel heard in the ending of butter or brother—does not exist in Sindarin. Avoid adding it after a final W.
03

Diphthongs

Two vowels glide together into one syllable.

AI

Begin with the “i” in fine or light, not the vowel in straight. See the finer distinction below.

AE

The “i” in fine or bright is a starting point; see the note on AI and AE.

AU / AW

Like “ou” in loud or “ow” in now.

EI

Like English “ay” in play or say.

OE

The “oy” sound in boy.

UI

Like “ui” in ruin, gliding the sounds together.

04

Sound combinations

ER

As in fair.

IR

As in dear or ear.

UR

As in poor.

In all three combinations, remember to keep the R trilled. English examples are approximations and vary with accent.
05

Accented vowels

Vowel accents indicate length. Moving from an unmarked vowel to an acute accent and then a circumflex, hold the sound progressively longer.

AShortest
ÁLonger
ÂLongest

An Â should have approximately twice the duration of an A. Length is about how long you sustain the vowel, not how loudly you say it.

06

Pronunciation of i

The short “i” described here follows linguistic notes published in Donald Swann’s The Road Goes Ever On: A Song Cycle. The source lesson treats these later notes as superseding the earlier guidance in the appendices of The Lord of the Rings.

“The short vowels may be rendered as in E. [English] sick, bed, hot, foot (for u), though o is intended to be rounder than in modern E.”LINGUISTIC NOTES · THE ROAD GOES EVER ON

Notice that this also gives foot as the reference for short U, a more specific distinction than the introductory “oo” approximation.

07

A further note on AI & AE

These sounds are difficult to describe using English alone. They should not be identical: start with the “i” sound in light or bright, then glide toward the second vowel.

AE → E

Glide toward Sindarin E, the vowel in fed.

AI → I

Glide toward Sindarin I, the vowel in sick (or the “ee” of machine for an accented I).

08

A further note on TH

When T and H meet across parts of a compound, they may be pronounced separately: a normal T followed by H, rather than the single TH sound.

Lanthir

Waterfall — Lant + sir.

Panthael

Fullwise — Pant + sael; a nickname for Samwise Gamgee.

09

Syllable stress

The following guidance summarises Tolkien’s explanation in Appendix E of The Lord of the Rings.

SyllablesWhere the stress falls
TwoOn the first syllable: EL-rond, AR-wen.
Three or moreOn the penultimate (second-to-last) syllable, or the antepenultimate (third-to-last) syllable.

Second-to-last syllable

Stress the penultimate syllable when it contains a long vowel or diphthong, or when its vowel is followed by two or more consonants.

i-SIL-dur · peri-ANN-ath

In Isildur, the I in SIL is short, but the following LD makes this syllable take the stress.

Third-to-last syllable

If the penultimate syllable has a short vowel followed by only one consonant or consonant sound, stress moves back one syllable. TH ordinarily counts as a single sound.

DE-ne-thor · BO-ro-mir · ga-LAD-ri-el

In Galadriel, I and E belong to separate syllables, not a diphthong. The short I in RI therefore sends the stress back to LAD.

A possible exception. In Tolkien’s own reading of A Elbereth Gilthoniel, he stresses Linnathon as LINN-a-thon. The reason is uncertain; use the general rules above as your starting point.
